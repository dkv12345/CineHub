# Art Direction — Cinematic Luxury

## Design thesis

Build a digital cinema brand that feels like entering a carefully curated screening room: quiet confidence, dramatic imagery, excellent typography, considered materials, and a frictionless ticketing experience.

The result should feel closer to a professionally art-directed cinema, culture, or hospitality brand than to a streaming clone, generic SaaS dashboard, or AI-generated landing page.

## Desired emotional qualities

- Cinematic, intimate, confident, crafted, editorial, tactile, contemporary.
- Premium through restraint and detail, not through gold decoration everywhere.
- Strong imagery, but not at the expense of clear navigation or booking.
- A little character and brand confidence; never sterile or generic.

## Three reference lenses

### Everyman — premium editorial and hospitality

Borrow the principles of curated programming, calm editorial hierarchy, brand-led content, considered whitespace, and the feeling that cinema is an experience rather than a commodity.

Do not copy its logo, exact layout, typography, or brand colors.

### Alamo Drafthouse — confident programming and useful discovery

Borrow the principles of clear film categories, location-aware discovery, decisive ticket calls-to-action, and a distinctive brand voice. Its strength is personality and content organization, not necessarily the exact dark/luxury palette.

Do not copy its proprietary visual identity or content.

### HOYTS — commercial clarity and booking usability

Borrow the principles of scalable movie discovery, clear session-time access, well-organized experiences/offers, and a booking process that lets people understand seat availability early.

Do not imitate its exact interface. Apply the underlying usability logic to the current product's real booking capabilities.

## The visual language

### Surfaces

- Primary canvas: deep charcoal or near-black, not pure #000000 everywhere.
- Elevated areas: subtly warmer/lighter charcoal; distinguish surfaces mainly through tone and thin borders.
- Text: warm off-white rather than harsh pure white.
- Supporting text: readable warm gray, never so dim that metadata becomes inaccessible.
- Accent: muted champagne/brass used sparingly for selected states, fine rules, small metadata, or a signature detail.
- Existing brand color takes precedence when one is already established. Use only one dominant accent.

### Composition

- Use a purposeful editorial grid with clear alignment.
- Let the feature film artwork carry the drama.
- Mix wide cinematic stills and portrait posters only where the content calls for each ratio.
- Use scale, crop, typography, whitespace, and alignment to create hierarchy.
- Avoid a monotonous wall of identical poster cards. Vary composition only when it improves hierarchy, not for novelty.
- Avoid full-bleed imagery behind dense text unless a readable contrast treatment is present.

### Texture and effects

- Prefer flat, carefully tuned surfaces, fine borders, and restrained shadows.
- If using a dark image overlay, use it only to preserve text contrast.
- No decorative neon, ambient blobs, animated glows, glass panels, or gradient mesh backgrounds.
- Avoid heavy blur and parallax. Interactions should be subtle and purposeful.

## Anti-AI visual rejection rules

Reject a design pass if it relies on any of the following without a specific functional reason:

- Purple/blue/pink gradient buttons or gradient text.
- Gradient-filled cards, gradient borders, or glowing card edges.
- Glassmorphism as the default surface style.
- Poppins/Inter/Space Grotesk applied automatically without typographic reasoning.
- Huge rounded pills for every button, filter, card, and input.
- The same rounded card repeated for every content section.
- Random floating circles, blurred blobs, sparkles, or abstract 3D decorations.
- Excessive drop shadows on dark surfaces.
- Overly large centered hero headline with generic marketing copy.
- Repeated eyebrow labels, tiny uppercase labels, and badges on every element.
- Emoji in place of a consistent icon system.
- Fake ratings, fabricated social proof, or invented “trending” numbers.
- Generic copy such as “Your ultimate cinematic journey starts here” unless it is genuinely part of the brand voice.

A gradient is not forbidden by physics, but it is not part of the default visual language. If a gradient is proposed, explain the functional/art-direction reason and use it only in a specific, restrained context. Never use gradient buttons as the default CTA.

## What “premium” must not become

- Low contrast.
- Tiny metadata.
- Excessive whitespace that pushes showtimes below the fold.
- Decorative complexity that slows booking.
- All-black layouts with no surface hierarchy.
- Gold everywhere.
- Serif fonts used for every label and form control.
- A desktop-first design that becomes cramped on mobile.

Premium means clarity, consistency, craft, and confidence.
